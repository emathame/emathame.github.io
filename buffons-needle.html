The Mathematics Behind Buffon's Needle

Suppose parallel lines are separated by distance D, and a needle has length L, with L ≤ D. The needle lands with a uniformly random position and direction.

The probability that it crosses a line is P(crossing) = 2L/(πD). In this experiment, L = D, so P(crossing) = 2/π ≈ 0.63662.

If N needles are dropped and C cross a line, then C/N estimates the crossing probability. Rearranging gives π ≈ 2N/C, provided C is not zero.

With many independent drops, the crossing proportion tends towards 2/π. This does not mean every larger sample must give a better estimate: random fluctuations still occur.

A mathematical challenge

Suppose 10,000 needles are dropped and 6,400 cross a line. What estimate of π does the experiment give? Why is it not exactly π?

Show Explanation

Using π ≈ 2N/C gives π ≈ 2 × 10,000 / 6,400 = 3.125. This differs from π because the number of crossings is random. A larger sample generally improves precision, but does not guarantee an exact result.

A deeper question

Why does π appear in a problem about dropping needles? The needle's angle is uniformly distributed, and the calculation involves the average of the absolute value of a sine function. Integrating over possible angles introduces π.
