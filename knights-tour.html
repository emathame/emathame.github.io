The Mathematics Behind This Puzzle

The Knight's Tour is a classical mathematical problem in which a knight must visit every square of a chessboard exactly once, using only legal knight moves.

What does graph theory tell us?

We can represent the chessboard as a graph. Each square becomes a vertex, and two vertices are connected by an edge whenever a knight can move directly between those squares.

A complete knight's tour is therefore a Hamiltonian path: a path that visits every vertex of a graph exactly once.

Open and closed tours

An open tour visits every square exactly once but does not finish a knight's move away from its starting square.

A closed tour also visits every square exactly once, but the knight can return to its starting square in one additional move. In graph theory, this corresponds to a Hamiltonian cycle.

Warnsdorff's rule

One useful strategy is to move the knight to the unvisited square that has the fewest onward moves available.

This is known as Warnsdorff's rule. The idea is to visit the most restricted squares early, rather than leaving them until they become impossible to reach.

Although the rule is remarkably effective, it does not guarantee success in every situation without additional choices or backtracking.

Why does board size matter?

The number and arrangement of squares affect whether a complete tour is possible. Some boards admit open tours but not closed tours.

For example, a 5 × 5 board admits open knight's tours, but no closed tour is possible.

A mathematical challenge

Can you explain why a closed knight's tour is impossible on any square chessboard with an odd number of squares?

Hint: A knight always moves from a light square to a dark square, or vice versa. Think about what happens when the knight returns to its starting square.

Show Explanation

Every knight move changes the colour of the square on which the knight stands.

To return to its starting square, a knight must therefore make an even number of moves.

A closed tour on an n × n board requires exactly n² moves, including the final move back to the starting square.

If n is odd, n² is also odd. But a knight cannot return to its starting square after an odd number of moves.

Therefore, a closed knight's tour is impossible on any odd-sized square board.
