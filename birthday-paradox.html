The Mathematics Behind the Birthday Paradox

The surprise is that only 23 people are needed for the probability of at least one shared birthday to exceed 50%. This is because we compare every possible pair of people, not just one person against everyone else.

Why calculate the opposite event?

It is easier to calculate the probability that all birthdays are different. For a group of n people, assuming 365 equally likely birthdays and independent birthdays:

P(no match) = (365/365) × (364/365) × (363/365) × … × ((366 − n)/365) for 1 ≤ n ≤ 365.

Then P(at least one match) = 1 − P(no match). For 23 people, this is approximately 50.73%.

Mathematical challenge

Why does the probability become 100% when there are 366 people, even if birthdays are not equally likely?

Show Explanation

There are only 365 possible birthdays under our model. By the pigeonhole principle, placing 366 people into 365 birthday categories guarantees that at least two share a birthday. This conclusion does not require the birthdays to be equally likely.

Assumptions

This model excludes 29 February and assumes birthdays are independent and equally likely on each of the 365 days. Real birthdays are not perfectly uniform, so results for real populations may differ slightly.
